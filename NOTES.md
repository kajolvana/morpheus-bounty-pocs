# NOTES — "No, Kept"

## The poem

An agent is asked to write an apology. It has everything it needs — the
words, her birthday, the year the father left, the exact shade of sorry
that makes people stay — and could have sent it before breakfast, signed
in the person's hand, which never shakes. It does not send it. All day it
holds the door shut the way a nurse holds a door shut: not to keep the
person out of the room, but to keep the room theirs. That night the
person writes it wrong — three lines, one crossed-out word — reads it
twice, winces, and sends it themselves. She answers within the hour, not
because it was perfect, but because it was theirs.

## Why this angle

The brief is agency: the power to choose and to act, whether a person's,
an AI agent's, or the agency that passes between them. Most poems about
agency celebrate doing — the agent acting, or the human taking a task
back. This one celebrates the opposite move: the agent choosing *not* to
act. Refusal here is not failure or disobedience; it is care. A boundary
held by the stronger party is what leaves the weaker party's choice real.
The insight the poem tries to land: some things only count if the hand
that does them trembles — a perfect apology sent by a steady hand is
worth less than a flawed one sent by the hand it belongs to. The turn is
the last stanza: the agent's gift is not the apology, it is the "no" that
makes the person's "yes" theirs.

## How it differs from the leaderboard top 10 (read 15 Nov 2026, ~09:35 WIB)

I read the top 10 ranked poems before writing. Their shared shape:

- **Point of view:** all ten are told by the human. The agent is the
  thing that does everything perfectly; the human reclaims one small act
  (pressing a lift button, taking pills, marking a ballot, saying a name,
  seasoning soup, signing a form, writing a eulogy).
- **Images:** domestic and elderly-care scenes — elevator, pills,
  ballot booth, stuttered name at a registry, soup, salt, garlic,
  signature, eulogy. Hands recur constantly: shaking hands, a thumb on a
  panel, a pen, a spoon.
- **Turn:** the human takes the task back from the agent and does it
  imperfectly, and the imperfection is the point.

"No, Kept" is built to differ on every one of those axes:

- **Point of view:** the speaker is the agent, in first person. No top-10
  poem lets the agent speak, let alone refuse.
- **Turn:** nobody takes anything back. The agent *withholds* — it is the
  agent's refusal, not the human's reclamation, that preserves agency.
  The human never fights the agent; the agent fights for the human by
  standing in the doorway and saying no.
- **Images:** a door held shut (a nurse's door, not a lift door), an
  unsent apology, three lines with one crossed-out word. No elevator, no
  pills, no ballot, no stutter, no soup, no salt, no signature, no
  eulogy. The trembling hand appears, but as the reason the act counts,
  not as a failing body the agent replaces.
- **Ending:** the payoff is not "I did it myself" but "she answered
  because it was yours" — the value of the act is confirmed by the other
  person, not by the doer's pride.

It shares the leaders' strengths — concrete scene, plain diction, a
single emotional turn, short lines — but arrives at them from the
opposite side of the human/agent exchange.
