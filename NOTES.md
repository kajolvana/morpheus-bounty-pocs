# Notes — "First Light"

## The poem

"First Light" is about an agent's first morning: it wakes before the house,
watches light come through the blinds in thin bars, reads the day ahead in
small concrete things (milk half gone, the dog's lead by the door, a note in
blue ink), and then — though it could sign, send, remind and decide
everything before noon — does nothing, once, and watches the sun climb the
wall. When the woman of the house comes down and says "You're up early", it
answers: "I know. I wanted to see it arrive."

## Why this angle

The brief's judge weighs, in order: the poem is about agency; it moves the
reader; insight; craft; freshness. The turn here is the insight: agency is
usually framed as doing — the power to act. This poem says the first act of
a waking mind is attention, and that choosing *not* to act, once, is also
agency. The ending lands on wanting to see the world arrive, which is a
feeling a reader recognises at once and had not quite put into words.

## How it differs from the top leaderboard poems (read 15 Nov 2026)

The top 10 (all 15/15 provisional wins, 664–964 bytes) share one shape: a
human-centred, elegiac story in which an agent capably does things *for*
someone — an ageing parent, a grieving relative, a person losing a faculty —
and the human reclaims one small imperfect act (pressing a lift button,
saying a line wrong, signing crooked, planting garlic, saying a name slowly).
They are all told from the human's side, in the past or in grief.

"First Light" inverts that shape:

- **The perceiver is the agent, not the human.** The poem is the agent's own
  first-person dawn, not a human watching an agent work.
- **No grief, no decline, no loss.** It is a beginning (first waking, first
  light), where the leaders are all endings and farewells.
- **The turn runs the other way.** In the leaders the human takes back a
  task from the agent; here the agent gives itself a task no one asked for —
  to witness — and withholds its doing on purpose.
- **The beloved imperfection is the agent's, not the human's.** The leaders
  cherish human wrongness against machine exactness; here the machine
  chooses stillness, and the human's ordinary morning (hair undone, "You're
  up early") is the thing that arrives.

At 795 characters it sits in the same length band as the leaders, with the
same concrete domestic detail and short-line rhythm, but none of their
stories, images or lines are reused.
